\subsubsection{Recording hippocampal \gsp{lfp} with \gs{deeg} in humans}
To characterize hippocampal network activity in humans, we recorded \gp{lfp} directly from the hippocampus using \g{deeg}. Thirty-five participants undergoing clinical monitoring for pharmacoresistant epilepsy in two centers (Toulouse and Paris) were included in this study. Electrode implantation followed clinical requirements only. Because the hippocampus is commonly involved in seizure networks (REF), electrodes were often implanted in the hippocampus (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-electrodes}A). In this manuscript, we focussed on participants with at least one electrode implanted in the hippocampus. Participants were implanted with standard and hybrid depth electrodes (DIXI Medical in Toulouse or Behnke–Fried in Paris). Hybrid electrodes incorporated both macrocontacts and microelectrodes (tetrodes or microwires). For consistency across the two centers, macrocontacts were used to obtain \gp{lfp} and common average referencing was applied. Tetrodes were used to identify single-neuron activity next to the macrocontact (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-electrodes}B) and are used from chapter~\ref{subsec:2Hz-neurons} of this manuscript. Anatomical localization of each macrocontact was obtained by co-registering postoperative CT with preoperative MRI and mapping contacts to individual hippocampal volumes (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-electrodes}C,D). For additional methodological details see Appendix~\ref{apdx:acquisition}.

\subsubsection{Hippocampal \gsp{lfp} are paced by a 2-Hz oscillation}
Analyzing brain oscillations often involves assuming that a given biological signal lies within a strict frequency range. This approach is efficient when this biological phenomenon is well established such as theta oscillations in mice (REF), but it constrains the analysis to prior knowledge. An alternative is to use unsupervised signal decomposition methods such as \g{emd}. \g{emd} decomposes \gp{lfp} into their constituent oscillatory components (referred to as \gp{imf}) without assuming fixed frequency bands (REF). However, several factors can influence the spectral structure of \gp{lfp} across macrocontacts and subjects. As a result, the extracted \gp{imf} may overlap in frequency (mode mixing) or may not be detected in a consistent manner across macrocontacts or participants (low consistency) (REF). These issues make it challenging to ensure that \gp{imf} are reliably identified across subjects. To address these limitations, we used a recently introduced version of masked \g{emd}, referred to as \gs{tmemd} (REF). This approach introduces controlled masking signals during decomposition to reduce mode mixing and to improve the separation of oscillatory components. In addition, mask parameters are optimized across subjects, which increases the consistency of the detected components at the group level. \gs{tmemd} therefore provides a more stable and interpretable decomposition of hippocampal \gp{lfp} than standard \g{emd}, particularly in datasets with substantial inter-individual variability. Full methodological details are provided in Appendix~\ref{apdx:acquisition}.

In participants who were awake and watching screen displays, we detected one oscillation in the human hippocampus with a peak frequency around 6 Hz (peak [80\% power band (PB)]: 6.15 [3.75 – 8.50] Hz). In addition, we identified two slower rhythms centered near 2 Hz [peak (80\% PB): 2.38 (1.25 – 3.50) Hz] and 1 Hz [peak (80\% PB): 1.08 (0.65 – 1.50) Hz] (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-imfs}).

This procedure allowed us to quantify narrow-band oscillatory peaks independently of differences in overall spectral slope across contacts or participants. After removing the aperiodic component, the periodic residuals showed a clear prominence of the 2-Hz component in the hippocampus (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-power_acrossFreqs}A). \subsubsection{Validation of 2-Hz oscillations}
\subsubsubsection{Phase reversal of hippocampal 2-Hz oscillations}
Hippocampal pyramidal cells are arranged in a well-defined laminar structure, which creates a consistent somato-dendritic axis that allows oscillations to be detected in \gp{lfp} (REF). When large groups of these neurons receive synchronized post-synaptic currents, the resulting transmembrane currents sum in space and form a macroscopic current dipole. This dipole generates extracellular voltage gradients that can be measured by depth electrodes as \gp{lfp}. Because this dipole is aligned across the pyramidal layer, \gp{lfp} recorded on linearly arranged contacts, i.e., spanning the somato-dendritic axis, would show a phase reversal: a same oscillatory cycle will have opposed phase on contacts located on opposite sides of the dipole. In the human hippocampus, layers are organized along the para-sagittal axis thus the dipole lies predominantly along the medio-lateral axis. The trajectories of the electrode shafts in our recordings positioned adjacent macrocontacts along this medio–lateral axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-anatomy}A, left anterior contacts and Fig.~\ref{fig:phaseReversal}A), where we observed a clear 2-Hz phase reversal (Fig.~\ref{fig:phaseReversal}B,C). This reversal is consistent with a locally generated oscillation rather than a volume-conducted signal from a distal source (see Chapter~\ref{subsec:2Hz-neurons} for further evidence).

\subsubsubsection{Local reference impairs detection of slow oscillations}
To investigate other factors that may impact the detectability of these slower oscillations, we compared two re-referencing montages commonly used with macrocontacts (\g{car} versus bipolar reference), and with microelectrodes (monopolar versus local reference). Bipolar and local reference involve subtracting signal from adjacent macrocontacts or tetrodes, respectively. Raw signals and in particular slow oscillations were dramatically affected by bipolar and local reference (Fig.~\ref{fig:localRef}A): they became either invisible or they showed inconsistent phase distortions. Across all hippocampal macrocontacts, bipolar/local reference impaired detectability of these slower oscillations (Fig.~\ref{fig:localRef}B--D). For this reason, all analyses in this manuscript use the \g{car}, which better preserves slow oscillatory components.

% ---------- Subsection 2: 2-Hz in memory task
\subsection{Hippocampal 2-Hz is selectively evoked in the memory task}

Across participants, hippocampal \gp{lfp} revealed a prominent 2-Hz oscillation that dominated other slow rhythms and occurred in transient bursts. This rhythm showed a clear laminar phase reversal and was strongest in contacts free of \gsp{ied}, suggesting that it is a physiological rhythm. These properties indicate that hippocampal 2-Hz could be a good candidate oscillation for comparison with rodent hippocampal theta. Hippocampal theta in rodents increases during exploratory behaviour, and during memory encoding and recall (REF). In rabbits, bats and cats it is also tightly linked with sensory acquisition and is thus considered a key mechanism of active sensing (REFS). This raises two questions for the comparison with human hippocampal oscillations: does hippocampal 2-Hz also increase during memory encoding and recall, and is it similarly evoked by memory-relevant cues?  

\subsubsection{Hippocampal 2-Hz power increase with mnemonic engagement}
\label{subsubsec:hpc-2Hz-memory}
To address this question we compared the changes in oscillatory power while participants were engaged in the associative memory task presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:behaviour}. Focussing on hippocampal macrocontacts free of \gp{ied}, we observed that 2-Hz oscillations exhibited significantly greater power during both learning and recall than during resting or viewing sessions (Fig.~\ref{fig:dep-powerTask-controls}; learning, P < 0.001; recalling, P = 0.006; paired permutation tests compared to pre-learning rest).

\subsubsection{Hippocampal 2-Hz bursts are evoked by mnemonic cues}

While hippocampal 2-Hz activity varies with overall mnemonic engagement of the participants, it remains unclear when these oscillatory bursts occur during the task. Because visual stimuli evoke strong \gp{erp} in the hippocampus, which manifest as broadband, non-oscillatory deflections in the \gp{lfp}, it is essential to separate these evoked responses from genuine oscillatory activity. Thus, to tease apart 2-Hz bursts from these evoked responses, we next analyze \gp{lfp} dynamics at different times relative to stimulus onset.

\subsubsubsection{\gsp{erp} are modulated by mnemonic engagement}
Previous work using hippocampal \g{deeg} has shown that the late \gp{erp} deflection occurring at 600 ms post stimulus onset (hP600) was deeper for familiar as opposed to novel stimuli (REF Barbeau). This suggests that hippocampal \gp{erp} are modulated by memory, which we first aimed at replicating in our behavioural task. For tis, we measured average \gp{lfp} after the onset of photographs in the viewing, learning and recalling phases of the memory task (Fig.~\ref{fig:erps-heatmap}).

% --------------- Section II: 2-Hz organizes neuronal and gamma activity in the HPC
\section{Hippocampal neuronal activity is preferentially modulated at 2-Hz}
Oscillations at 2 Hz can be observed in the hippocampal \gp{lfp}. These oscillations are transient, and selectively evoked by mnemonic cues. However, 2-Hz is a particularly slow rhythm in comparison to the timescale of the cellular (ensemble spiking activity) and local network (gamma oscillations) processes at play in the hippocampus (REF). To solve this apparent paradox, we next investigated whether 2-Hz oscillations modulate ensemble spiking and gamma activity locally within the hippocampus.

% ---------- Subsection 1: Hippocampal neurons
\subsection{Hippocampal neurons are paced at 2-Hz}
\label{subsec:2Hz-neurons}

\subsubsection{Basic firing properties of hippocampal neurons reveal 2-Hz rhythmicity}
Using tetrode \gp{lfp} recordings, we were able to isolate single-neuron activity from the human hippocampus while participants were engaged in the associative memory task as well as watching a sitcom series (see Appendix~\ref{apdx:acquisition} for details on the datasets and Appendix~\ref{apdx:spike-sorting} for isolation of single-units). In the hippocampus, 211 neurons were identified from 18 participants. Because tetrodes protruded between the two deepest macrocontacts, neurons were mostly sampled from the medial parts of the hippocampus (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-neuronsBasic}A). The majority of neurons detected had a low firing rate, below 5 Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-neuronsBasic}B) and broad spiking waveforms (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-neuronsBasic}C-E), consistent with the proportion of pyramidal neurons observed in the rodent hippocampus (REF).

\subsubsection{Hippocampal neurons prefer 2-Hz oscillations}
\label{subsubsec:2Hz-ppc}
If hippocampal neurons are entrained by the 2-Hz oscillation, their spike trains should preferentially lock to the 2-Hz phase than to the phase of other slow-frequency oscillations. We assessed this coupling by measuring \g{ppc} at 1-, 2- and 6-Hz using the \gp{imf} detected from the macrocontact adjacent to the tetrodes (c). The observed \g{ppc} values were then compared with a null distribution obtained by randomly permuting the phase of each \g{imf} in the Fourier domain (see Appendix~\ref{apdx:spike-field} for methodological details). This measure allowed us to compare coupling across frequencies while controlling for potential power-law confounds (REF). Single-neurons locking was higher for 2-Hz than 1- or 6-Hz oscillations in the hippocampus (Fig.~\ref{thefig_hpc-neuronsPPC}). Hence, this result confirms that the 2-Hz rhythm organizes single-neuron activity locally in the hippocampus.

% ---------- Subsection 2: Hippocampal gamma
\subsection{Hippocampal gamma oscillations are preferentially modulated at 2-Hz}
\subsubsection{Hippocampal gamma activity is preferentially coupled to 2-Hz phase}
In rodents, gamma oscillations reflect the activity of local networks, namely the interaction between pyramidal neurons and interneurons (REF). First, we confirmed that neuronal spiking correlated with local gamma-band (60 – 160 Hz) activity in humans as well (see Appendix~\ref{apdx:spike2Gamma} and REFS). In addition, rodent gamma oscillations couples with theta (REF), and this theta-gamma \g{pac} is crucial for memory acquisition and recall (REFS). Consequently, we then tested whether gamma-band activity was coupled to hippocampal 2-Hz. By measuring the average of gamma activity and population firing rate triggered by individual oscillatory cycles of 1-, 2- and 6-Hz oscillations, we showed clear entrainment of gamma activity at 2-Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:hpc-trigAvgs}).

% --------------- Section III: 2-Hz organizes neuronal and gamma activity in the MTL
\section{Hippocampal 2-Hz synchronizes neuronal activity across \gs{mtl} regions}
In rodents, theta oscillations synchronize neuronal activity in various, distributed brain regions including the entorhinal cortex, amygdala, prefrontal cortex, medial septum, and locus coeruleus (REFS). In humans, the \g{mtl} shares dense anatomical connections with the hippocampus (REF), making it a suitable candidate for functional connectivity at 2 Hz. To illustrate this, we used structural data available online in open access (REF Škoch) and obtained from \g{dwmri} in 88 human participants. Because of the visual nature of our task, we focussed on the connectivity of the hippocampus with regions located along the ventral visual stream: \g{mtl}, temporal and occipital cortices. The hippocampus showed preferential connectivity with the entorhinal and parahippocampal cortices, followed by amygdala, temporal pole and fusiform gyrus (Fig.~\ref{fig:structural-connectivity}), confirming the relevance of \g{mtl} structures to study functional connectivity with the hippocampus at 2 Hz.

\chapter{Discussion}

Limits: what happens out of the oscillatory bursts? Noise does not exist in physiology: what information do these out-of-bursts epochs carry? These would be epochs where the firing rate is higher (aperiodic components), fractal measures are higher, but it remains unclear what is actually happening there for the network. Is it really "not" communicating with other structures? If yes, how does it communicate? What are the alternatives mechanisms to "communication through coherence"? Main challenge to study this is that the rodent hippocampus is virtually always paced by theta. Then maybe that would be the biggest inter-species difference we find: transient oscillations. Note: it could be useful to illustrate this discussion to show 6-8Hz in the temporal cortex as well.
